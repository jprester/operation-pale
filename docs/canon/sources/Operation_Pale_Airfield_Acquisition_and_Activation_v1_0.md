# Operation: Pale — Airfield Acquisition and Activation Doctrine v1.0

*Surface-logistics doctrine after the Day-Two airfield-access revision*

**Canon status:** current working doctrine under Canon Handoff v2.4.  
**Purpose:** describe how the Company obtains and converts existing human airfields without using Kestrels as assault craft.

## 1. Why airfields matter

The Company's opening atmospheric force is deliberately mixed.

- **Merlins** solve the first one to two hours of low/medium-altitude interception, now including direct Quiver-M deployment.
- **Storks** provide reusable theatre-level Merlin carriage, redistribution, and utility support after H-hour.
- **Harrows** remain airborne for days but eventually need recovery, inspection, repair, battery conditioning, wing cleaning, and payload replacement.
- **Strato-Hawks** come later and require genuinely secure runway infrastructure.
- **Kestrels** do not require a conventional runway, but their safe vertical landing pads work best beside an already-qualified logistics hub.

Therefore, the Company does not need to occupy whole countries before it can operate. It needs a **distributed network of cooperative, observable, technically usable aviation sites**.

## 2. Political acquisition principle

The Company seeks **operational access**, not immediate sovereignty.

Preferred counterparties:

1. recognized national government;
2. joint national/regional authority;
3. regional or local authority that demonstrably controls the nominated site when the national state cannot or will not provide one.

The Company avoids automatically recognizing separatist governments because fragmentation increases administrative workload.

Standard clause:

> Operational control of this site is recognized. No determination concerning territorial sovereignty or constitutional status is implied.

Airfield access purchases services and access; it does not automatically purchase border changes, regime protection, weapons, or domestic political victory.

## 3. Airfield classes

### Class A — emergency recovery strip

Purpose: safe landing and basic turnaround during late Day One / early Day Two.

Minimum requirements:

- usable runway or hard-surface strip;
- cleared approach;
- local fire response;
- tested fuel if Storks are expected;
- basic Company navigation beacon;
- compact robotic inspection team;
- one secure parking area;
- local authority capable of excluding armed interference.

No Kestrel is required.

### Class B — Stork/Harrow forward operating field

Adds:

- persistent Nest;
- Merlin unloading/reload handling;
- engine and fuel-system diagnostics;
- Harrow maintenance frames;
- payload handling;
- sheltered spares;
- continuous Rook/Swift/Midge/Stake security;
- integrated human/robot fuel and utility support.

### Class C — regional Company aviation hub

Adds:

- multiple Stork stands;
- Harrow servicing capacity;
- higher-capacity power and communications;
- local Quern/Forge capability;
- larger stores;
- dedicated secure logistics zone;
- civil/humanitarian traffic integration.

### Class D — Kestrel strategic gateway

Adds a separate VTOL pad and sterile approach corridor. A Kestrel lands only after the site has repeatedly proven control and safe operations.

## 4. Activation sequence

### Phase 1 — nomination

The human authority provides:

- runway/taxiway diagrams;
- fuel types and inventories;
- power and water connections;
- existing aircraft and weapons locations;
- fire/rescue status;
- hazards and damaged areas;
- nearby population map;
- one accountable site commander;
- proposed security perimeter and access roads.

Lantern and local sensors compare the declaration with observable reality.

### Phase 2 — site freeze

Before any reusable Company aircraft lands:

- military aircraft are parked and declared;
- weapons are removed from immediate use;
- air-defence radar is shut down;
- missiles/ammunition are declared and separated;
- fuel vehicles are parked in known locations;
- runway/taxiways are cleared;
- emergency services remain staffed;
- local security holds a perimeter;
- no unexplained military movement occurs.

The important test is behavioral: can the authority issue an order at the site and make it stick?

### Phase 3 — first expendable packages

The Company sends low-value disposable Casks or equivalent delivery packages rather than a Kestrel.

A typical first set contains:

**Control/power package**
- compact Nest core;
- optical communications;
- navigation beacons;
- batteries/power conditioning;
- weather/environment sensors.

**Reconnaissance package**
- Midges;
- Crawlers;
- Stakes;
- Ferrets;
- chemical and structural inspection tools.

**Security package**
- Stingers;
- Rook point defence;
- Swift interceptors;
- limited Razors if the threat warrants them.

**Engineering package**
- Hands;
- light Forge capability;
- runway sweepers;
- cutting/lifting equipment;
- fuel samplers and filters;
- standardized pipe/hose/electrical interfaces.

Packages should land on an unused apron or nearby clear area rather than risk blocking the runway.

## 5. Robotic site survey

### Midges

Perform rapid surface mapping of:

- personnel;
- vehicles;
- roofs and towers;
- parked aircraft;
- hangars;
- antennas;
- open doors;
- perimeter activity.

### Crawlers

Inspect:

- drainage;
- culverts;
- cable ducts;
- under-vehicle spaces;
- fuel trenches;
- landing-gear pits;
- blast walls;
- maintenance voids.

### Ferrets

Enter confined or hazardous spaces:

- fuel vaults;
- utility tunnels;
- damaged buildings;
- ventilation;
- underground rooms;
- suspicious packages;
- sewer/drain access.

### Stakes

Establish persistent observation around:

- runway approaches;
- fence lines;
- roads;
- fuel farms;
- ammunition stores;
- vegetation and high ground;
- likely mortar/FPV launch positions.

The long-term threat to a Company airfield is less likely to be a conventional fighter than a small team with a cheap drone, mortar, concealed observer, or simple explosive.

## 6. The Nest

The Nest becomes the Company operating core for the site.

Functions:

- command and communications;
- charging/power;
- authentication;
- navigation reference;
- local sensor fusion;
- security coordination;
- drone maintenance;
- spares and ammunition;
- weather monitoring;
- liaison records.

The Nest does **not** need to replace the human control tower. The airfield operates with two interlocked layers:

**Human layer:** controllers, firefighters, fuel technicians, mechanics, airport management, utilities, warehouses, road access, local security.

**Company layer:** autonomous navigation, aircraft servicing, robotic security, machine authentication, precision maintenance, optical links to Lantern/Warden networks.

## 7. Hands and Forges

### Hands — human-infrastructure adapters

Hands exist because airports, refineries, vehicles, and maintenance equipment are designed for human limbs and interfaces.

Typical jobs:

- drive or operate fuel trucks;
- connect hoses;
- open valves;
- use human control panels;
- operate forklifts and tow tractors;
- use cranes and maintenance stands;
- open hangar doors;
- move pallets;
- operate pumps and generators;
- perform tool-level inspections.

Humans often continue running the upstream system while Hands perform the final machine-facing interface.

### Forges — heavy conversion

Typical jobs:

- clear wreckage;
- cut destroyed aircraft into movable sections;
- repair taxiways;
- move damaged concrete slabs;
- fill or bridge craters;
- emplace runway mats;
- move transformers;
- stabilize structures;
- build barriers and blast walls;
- trench protected cables;
- construct the Kestrel pad.

The Company normally chooses intact secondary fields where possible rather than rebuilding the most heavily attacked primary base.

## 8. Runway qualification

A runway is instrument-tested, not visually declared safe.

Checklist:

1. high-resolution surface map;
2. foreign-object/debris sweep;
3. subsurface void and structural check;
4. instrumented load pass;
5. rapid repair or bypass of damaged slabs;
6. independent Company navigation beacon installation;
7. full approach/obstacle survey;
8. sterile abort and diversion geometry;
9. emergency-response drill.

Human runway lights or ILS can remain useful, but Company aircraft do not depend on them.

## 9. Fuel system conversion for Storks

Storks should be deliberately compatible with terrestrial kerosene/heavy aviation fuel families within a broad tolerance.

Robotic qualification includes:

- sample every storage tank;
- identify water, sediment, microbial contamination, and additives;
- isolate bad tanks;
- flush or bypass suspect lines;
- install fine filtration and water separation;
- calibrate pumps;
- fit standardized couplings;
- certify batches for the expected engine mode.

Inferior but usable terrestrial fuel may impose lower performance and shorter maintenance intervals rather than making the aircraft unusable.

The important operational point is that Earth already has enormous fuel storage and distribution capacity. The Company imports **precision interfaces**, not ordinary kerosene.

## 10. Harrow servicing

Harrow does not require conventional refuelling.

Typical ground work:

- clean and inspect solar surfaces;
- structural inspection of ultra-light wings;
- swap damaged cell sections;
- battery/supercapacitor conditioning;
- motor and actuator replacement;
- payload-cassette loading;
- navigation/data update;
- minor composite repair.

A Harrow field can therefore be much more austere than a full Stork field.

## 11. Security zoning

### Outer zone — human controlled

Roads, public access, local police/security, community interaction.

### Shared logistics zone

Fuel, warehouses, firefighting, utilities, cargo, runway maintenance. Humans and Hands work together.

### Inner sterile zone

Nest core, Stork/Harrow servicing, Merlin stores, precision spares, Company data systems. Human access is controlled.

### Kestrel zone

Separate VTOL pad with debris control, thermal margin, protected transfer route, Rook/Swift defence, and a sterile approach corridor.

## 12. First aircraft sequence

1. **Harrow test landing** — low-risk reusable proof of navigation, runway, command, and perimeter discipline.
2. **Utility Stork** — arrives lightly loaded, receives terrestrial fuel, undergoes inspection, and departs.
3. **Operational Stork** — arrives for normal Merlin reload and turnaround.
4. **Kestrel** — only after repeated site success and security qualification.

## 13. Human labor remains central

The Company does not replace the entire airport workforce because that would waste both robots and local expertise.

**Humans are best at:** local infrastructure knowledge, fuel systems, firefighting, bulk labor, utilities, suppliers, warehouses, civil engineering, administration, local security.

**Company robots are best at:** trust verification, hazardous inspection, precision interfaces, high-speed repair, autonomous aircraft handling, sabotage detection, and access to orbital logistics.

The result is not a robot base dropped onto Earth. It is a **human airport with a Company machine layer grafted onto it**.

## 14. Political consequences

The first qualified airfields become the first visible material benefits of cooperation.

Hosts gain:

- protected cargo traffic;
- emergency power/fuel assistance;
- direct communications;
- medical and engineering support;
- early aviation restoration;
- infrastructure repair;
- regional diplomatic significance.

This makes a successful site politically sticky. Removing it later would also remove services, jobs, transport priority, and access.

The Company therefore does not need to conquer the first runway. It makes hosting one economically and administratively attractive enough that multiple authorities compete to qualify.
